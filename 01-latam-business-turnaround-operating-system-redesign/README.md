# EF Education First | LATAM Business Turnaround

### Rebuilding the system 

**Company:** EF Education First  
**Role:** Managing Director  
**Geography:** LATAM  
**Business:** International education across B2C, B2B, and B2G  
**Mandate:** Full P&L leadership and business turnaround  
**Focus:** Leadership | Operations | GTM | Revenue | Partnerships | Customer Experience | Profitability

---

# The Situation

I returned to EF to lead a LATAM business that had lost momentum.

Revenue was declining.

Lead flow and lead quality were down.

Conversion was low.

The sales cycle was too long.

Customer acquisition costs were high.

Churn was high.

Customer experience needed attention.

Several important partnership channels had weakened.

But the biggest issue was not sales.

It was how the business was being managed.

EF operated through a complex global matrix.

Several functions at HQ could influence the markets.

There were many opinions, different priorities, and unclear ownership.

Communication between HQ and the region was not working well enough.

Decisions were slow.

Local leaders did not always have the authority they needed.

Sales, marketing, operations, and customer experience were not working as one team.

Parts of the management layer were underperforming.

People needed more clarity about where the business was going, what mattered, and what they owned.

My conclusion was simple:

> **This was not a sales problem. It was a system problem.**

Selling harder was not going to fix it.

We needed to change how the business worked.

# What I Needed to Fix

I grouped the turnaround around six areas.

| Area | What Needed to Change |
| --- | --- |
| Leadership | Clearer expectations, stronger managers, real accountability |
| Organization | Better communication, clearer decisions, more local ownership |
| GTM | Better ICP definition, segmentation, localization, and focus |
| Revenue | Stronger demand, funnel discipline, conversion, pricing, and speed |
| Distribution | Rebuild K12, Higher Education, agents, corporate channels, and local coverage |
| Customer | Improve experience, retention, feedback, and post-sale performance |

Underneath all six sat the same management basics:

**Clear priorities.**

**Clear owners.**

**Clear KPIs.**

**Regular reviews.**

**Faster decisions.**

**Follow-through.**

---

# 1. Reset Leadership

The business could not change without the leadership team changing with it.

I assessed managers against practical questions:

Do they understand the business?

Do they own results?

Can they make decisions?

Do they use data?

Can they lead through change?

Can they develop their team?

Can they work across functions?

Do they solve problems or simply escalate them?

Not every manager was going to make the transition.

Three underperforming mid-level managers were ultimately removed.

Other responsibilities were reassigned.

Parts of the management layer were rebuilt.

People were moved closer to areas where they could create more business value.

The objective was not to change people for the sake of change.

It was to build a leadership team capable of running the business differently.

One rule became important:

> **One important outcome. One accountable owner.**

Many people could contribute.

But somebody had to own the result.

---

# 2. Make the Matrix Work Better

The matrix itself was not the enemy.

The problem was how we were operating inside it.

Too many decisions were influenced by multiple stakeholders.

That created a familiar pattern:

**Different opinions → Slow decisions → Unclear ownership → Weak execution**

We needed clearer boundaries.

For important decisions, we started asking:

Who owns the outcome?

Who makes the decision?

Who needs to contribute?

Who needs to know?

We also worked toward a simpler split between global and local responsibility.

| HQ / Global | LATAM / Local |
| --- | --- |
| Brand | Local execution |
| Core product standards | ICP priorities |
| Financial governance | Commercial priorities |
| Technology platforms | Channel mix |
| Corporate policies | Partnerships |
| Strategic guardrails | Local demand generation |
| Reporting standards | Sales execution |
| Global capabilities | Customer response |

The principle was simple:

> **Keep global what gives us scale. Keep local what helps us win the market.**

Local ownership increased.

So did accountability.

---

# 3. Improve the Conversation With HQ

The region also needed to communicate differently with HQ.

Instead of escalating:

**Here is the problem. What should we do?**

The conversation became:

**Here is what is happening.**

**Here is what the data tells us.**

**Here is what we believe is causing it.**

**Here are the options.**

**Here is our recommendation.**

**Here is what we need from HQ.**

That changed the quality of the relationship.

It reduced unnecessary escalation.

It also forced the regional team to take greater ownership.

The objective was not independence from HQ.

It was better regional leadership.

---

# 4. Redesign GTM Around Clearer ICPs

The commercial team needed a better answer to a basic question:

> **Who exactly are we trying to win?**

We sharpened ICP and segmentation across priority audiences including:

- K12
- Higher Education
- Young professionals
- Corporate relationships
- Other relevant customer groups

This affected much more than marketing.

The customer we chose affected:

**Messaging → Channel → Campaign → Sales Approach → Pricing → Partnerships → Customer Experience**

The goal was to stop spreading resources too widely and focus on the customers and channels where we had the strongest opportunity.

---

# 5. Improve Demand Quality

The business did not simply need more leads.

It needed better leads.

At the start:

**Lead flow was down approximately 30%.**

**SQL quality was down approximately 30%.**

We looked at:

- Who we were targeting
- Which campaigns were working
- Which channels created quality demand
- Which channels created volume with little value
- Whether marketing and sales agreed on what a good lead looked like
- Which sources actually converted into customers

The question changed from:

**How many leads did we generate?**

to:

> **Are we generating demand that can convert into profitable business?**

---

# 6. Rebuild the Commercial Engine

Once demand entered the business, the sales process needed to become much clearer.

The basic flow became:

**Lead → Qualified Opportunity → Pipeline → Decision → Customer → Retention**

For each stage, we wanted to know:

What needs to happen?

Who owns it?

How long should it take?

Where are customers dropping?

Why are they dropping?

What are we going to change?

That made the funnel useful for management, not just reporting.

## Lead Response

Response time moved from approximately:

**5 days → 24 hours**

This mattered because slow response affected everything after it.

Faster response improved the probability of engagement, conversion, and faster revenue.

## Conversion

Conversion moved from approximately:

**1.5% → 3%**

Roughly double the starting level.

But conversion was not treated as a sales-only problem.

We looked at:

- ICP
- Lead quality
- Channel
- Response time
- Sales capability
- Process
- Pricing
- Payment options
- Follow-up
- Customer need

If conversion dropped, we wanted to understand why before deciding what to do.

## Sales Cycle

The sales cycle moved from approximately:

**3 months → 6 weeks**

We looked for friction rather than simply asking salespeople to push harder.

What was slowing the customer?

Where were opportunities stopping?

Was follow-up weak?

Was pricing unclear?

Were payment structures creating friction?

Were customers getting the right information at the right time?

Removing friction improved speed.

## Acquisition Economics

CAC moved from approximately:

**22% → 12%**

I did not treat CAC as a marketing metric.

It was the result of:

**Targeting + Channel + Marketing + Sales + Conversion + Customer Value**

The goal was not simply to make acquisition cheaper.

It was to make acquisition economics better.

---

# 7. Rebuild Partnerships and Distribution

Direct acquisition could not be the only source of growth.

Several important channels had weakened.

We worked to rebuild:

- Higher Education
- K12
- Agent networks
- Corporate relationships
- Local market coverage

The agent vertical needed particular attention because it was underperforming.

Across all partnerships, I wanted the same logic:

**Access → Qualified Demand → Customers → Revenue → Economics**

The number of meetings or agreements was not enough.

The channel needed to create value.

Partnership ownership became clearer.

Relationships were rebuilt.

Coverage increased.

Some physical market presence was reactivated where there was a clear commercial case.

Over time:

> **Partnerships contributed approximately 18% to 20% of revenue.**

This gave the business more than one route to growth.

---

# 8. Look for New Sources of Growth

The turnaround was not only about fixing what was broken.

We also looked at underdeveloped business units, customer segments, partnerships, and commercial opportunities.

The question was:

> **Where else can we create profitable growth using capabilities we already have or can realistically build?**

Not every opportunity deserved investment.

We looked for:

- Real demand
- A clear customer
- Clear ownership
- Attractive economics
- Capability to execute
- Repeatability

An interesting idea is not automatically a business opportunity.

That distinction helped us stay focused.

---

# 9. Make Customer Experience Part of Revenue

The business was also losing too much value after acquisition.

At the start:

**NPS was approximately 35.**

**Churn was approximately 22%.**

Customer experience needed to become part of the commercial conversation.

We made customer feedback more visible.

Customer surveys were introduced.

Recurring problems were brought into management reviews.

Sales and post-sale performance became more connected.

Over time:

**NPS increased to approximately 76.**

**Churn decreased to approximately 12%.**

The logic was simple:

> **Revenue you keep matters as much as revenue you win.**

---

# 10. Build a Simple KPI System

One of the most important changes was moving from reporting numbers to managing them.

The basic logic became:

**Outcome → Driver → KPI → Owner → Review → Decision → Action**

The numbers needed to tell us where to look.

| KPI | Management Question |
| --- | --- |
| Lead Flow | Do we have enough demand? |
| SQL Quality | Are we attracting the right customers? |
| Lead Response | Are we responding fast enough? |
| Pipeline | Do we have enough future business? |
| Conversion | Where are we losing customers? |
| Sales Cycle | What is slowing the customer decision? |
| CAC | Are we acquiring customers efficiently? |
| NPS | What are customers telling us? |
| Churn | Why are customers leaving? |
| Partner Contribution | Which channels are creating value? |
| Revenue | Are we delivering the plan? |
| Profitability | Is growth creating economic value? |

The rule was:

> **Do not manage the number. Manage what produces the number.**

If conversion fell, we diagnosed the funnel.

If CAC increased, we looked at channel economics and conversion.

If churn increased, we looked at the customer journey.

A KPI was useful only if it helped us make a better decision.

---

# 11. Install a Management Rhythm

The business also needed consistency.

We introduced a simple operating cadence.

## Weekly

Focus on execution.

- Pipeline
- Lead flow
- Response
- Conversion
- Sales activity
- CRM quality
- Immediate blockers
- Previous commitments

Core question:

> **What needs attention now?**

## Monthly

Focus on performance.

- Revenue
- Conversion
- CAC
- Churn
- NPS
- Partnerships
- Team performance
- Resources
- Performance versus plan

Core question:

> **Where are we off plan, why, and what are we changing?**

## Quarterly

Focus on the business.

- Market performance
- Priorities
- Resource allocation
- GTM
- Leadership
- Business units
- Partnerships
- Structural issues
- What to stop
- What to scale

Core question:

> **Are we still doing the right things in the right way?**

The objective was not more meetings.

It was better management.

---

# 12. Use Technology Where It Helped

Technology was useful only if it solved a real operating problem.

First, CRM discipline needed to improve.

The data had to become trustworthy.

Pipeline stages needed to mean something.

Managers needed visibility.

Once that foundation improved, AI-enabled prioritization was introduced in Salesforce to help the commercial team focus on stronger opportunities.

The goal was not to say we were using AI.

The goal was:

**Better prioritization.**

**Better productivity.**

**Better decisions.**

Technology supported the team.

It did not replace management judgment.

---

# 13. Build a Business That Could Run Without Me

One risk in any turnaround is that everything starts depending on the person leading it.

I did not want that.

The objective was to build a management team that could increasingly run the business without requiring my involvement in every decision.

That meant:

- Clear ownership
- More decision authority where appropriate
- Coaching through real problems
- Regular performance reviews
- Better commercial understanding
- Stronger cross-functional work
- Better management habits

The goal was not for me to become the system.

> **The goal was to build a system the organization could run.**

---

# 14. Allocate Resources Around the Business Priorities

Running the turnaround also meant making choices about where we put people, budget, and management attention.

We could not fix everything at once.

And not every activity deserved the same level of investment.

I looked at the business through a few practical questions:

- Where is the strongest opportunity?
- What is producing results?
- What is consuming resources without enough return?
- Which channels should we rebuild?
- Which capabilities are missing?
- Where do we need stronger people?
- What should we stop or reduce?
- Where does local market reality justify a different approach?

Those questions influenced decisions across teams, channels, partnerships, territories, and physical market coverage.

Resources were moved closer to the priorities that could improve performance.

Responsibilities were reassigned where we needed stronger commercial contribution.

Partnership channels were rebuilt where they could create productive demand.

Physical presence was selectively reactivated where there was a clear business case.

Activities that could not demonstrate enough value were challenged.

The objective was not simply to spend less.

It was to use the resources we had better.

## Growth Had to Earn Investment

I did not want growth decisions based only on volume.

A channel, initiative, or business opportunity needed to make sense commercially.

The basic questions were:

**Is there real demand?**

**Can we win?**

**Do we have the capability to execute?**

**What resources will it require?**

**What return can it produce?**

**Can it become repeatable?**

This also meant making trade-offs.

More investment in one area meant less somewhere else.

More management attention on one priority meant deliberately giving less attention to another.

That is part of running a business.

The goal was to keep resources connected to the priorities that mattered most.

> **Strategy becomes real when you decide where the people, money, and management attention

Better leadership improved accountability.

Better accountability improved execution.

Clearer ICPs improved demand quality.

Better demand improved conversion.

Faster response improved sales velocity.

Stronger partnerships increased distribution.

**Commercial**

Rebuilding GTM, ICP, demand, pipeline, conversion, partnerships, pricing, and revenue.

Improving how HQ, regional leadership, and local teams worked together.

# What I Took From the Turnaround

A few principles stayed with me.

**Start with the real problem.**

The visible issue is not always the cause.

**Make ownership clear.**

If everyone owns something, nobody owns it.

**Get close to the operation.**

When the answer is in the detail, go into the detail.

**Use numbers to ask better questions.**

A dashboard is not management.

**Connect the functions.**

Customers do not care how the organization chart is structured.

**Move decisions closer to the problem.**

Within clear limits.

**Build leaders.**

The business should not depend permanently on one executive.

**Keep adjusting.**

Execution will tell you things the original plan could not.

---

# The Operating Principle

> **When a business is underperforming, do not start by pushing harder. Start by understanding what is producing the result.**

Make the problem visible.

Find what is getting in the way.

Clarify ownership.

Change what needs to change.

Execute.

Measure.

Learn.

Adjust.

Then repeat.
