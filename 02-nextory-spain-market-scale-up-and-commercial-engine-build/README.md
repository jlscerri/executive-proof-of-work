# Nextory | Spain Market Scale-Up & Commercial Engine Build

Building the commercial infrastructure required to scale a newly integrated subscription business in Spain.

**Company:** Nextory  
**Role:** Country Manager, Spain  
**Geography:** Spain  
**Business:** Digital subscription platform for ebooks and audiobooks  
**Mandate:** Scale the Spanish market and build the local commercial engine  
**Focus:** Market Scale-Up | GTM | B2B2C | Strategic Partnerships | Distribution | Customer Acquisition | Retention | Commercial Operations

---

## Executive Snapshot

Nextory had already entered Spain through the acquisition of Nubico.

The acquisition provided an existing customer base, content catalogue, and established relationships with Grupo Planeta and Telefónica.

But acquisition did not equal scale.

The local business still had a limited commercial infrastructure.

B2B distribution beyond the inherited relationships was underdeveloped.

Customer acquisition was expensive.

Churn was high.

Conversion needed improvement.

Lifecycle management was limited.

And the business needed a stronger local capability to turn the acquired platform into a scalable operation.

My mandate was therefore not to launch Spain from zero.

It was to build what came next.

The challenge was to develop the commercial engine required to scale the market.

That meant working across:

- GTM and localization
- B2C acquisition
- B2B and B2B2C distribution
- Strategic partnerships
- Pricing and packaging
- Customer activation
- Retention
- Customer economics
- Local commercial capability
- Commercial operations

One decision became particularly important.

Competing for every customer through direct digital acquisition was not enough.

Spain needed additional routes to market.

The commercial model therefore evolved toward a broader system:

**Direct Acquisition + Strategic Partnerships + B2B2C Distribution + Telecom + Customer Lifecycle**

The objective was not simply to acquire more users.

It was to build a more scalable way of acquiring, activating, and retaining them.

> **The product already existed. The next challenge was building the commercial system around it.**

## 02. The Starting Point

Nextory had already entered Spain through the acquisition of Nubico.

That gave the business a starting position.

There was an existing customer base.

There was an established content catalogue.

And there were inherited relationships with Grupo Planeta and Telefónica.

But the commercial engine around the product was still limited.

The challenge was no longer entering the market.

The challenge was scaling it.

At the starting point, several constraints were visible:

- Customer churn was approximately 42%
- Customer acquisition cost was high
- Conversion needed improvement
- B2B distribution beyond the inherited relationships was limited
- The enterprise model was underdeveloped
- Customer lifecycle automation was limited
- The local organization was small
- The business remained too dependent on direct customer acquisition

The inherited partnerships were valuable.

But two established relationships did not constitute a diversified distribution strategy.

If the business wanted to scale, it needed more ways to reach customers.

It also needed to improve what happened after acquisition.

Acquiring customers without improving activation, engagement, and retention would simply increase the cost of growth.

That created two connected challenges:

**Build stronger distribution.**

And:

**Improve the economics of the customer journey.**

---

## 03. The Commercial Diagnosis

The obvious response to a growing subscription business could have been to invest more heavily in direct customer acquisition.

I did not believe that was enough.

Spain was a competitive subscription market.

Large international platforms could compete aggressively for digital attention.

Trying to scale primarily by buying more traffic would leave the business increasingly dependent on acquisition spend.

The question therefore became:

**How can we reach more customers without depending primarily on direct acquisition?**

That changed how I looked at the market.

Instead of thinking only about:

**Nextory → Customer**

I looked for organizations that already had access to the customers we wanted.

That opened a different route:

**Nextory → Partner → Customer**

Potential partners already had:

- Large customer bases
- Existing customer relationships
- Distribution
- Communication channels
- Loyalty programs
- Membership propositions
- Established brands
- Reasons to add value to their own customers

Nextory had something those organizations could potentially use:

A digital content proposition that could be bundled, distributed, rewarded, or incorporated into a broader customer offer.

That created the basis for a B2B2C model.

But distribution alone would not solve the entire problem.

The customer economics also needed attention.

The commercial system therefore had to address the full journey:

**Reach → Acquisition → Activation → Engagement → Retention → Customer Value**

This became the scale-up thesis.

Do not treat acquisition, partnerships, retention, and customer economics as separate initiatives.

Build them as one commercial system.

> **The question was not simply how to acquire more customers. It was how to build more efficient and repeatable routes to customers.**

---

## 04. The Scale-Up Thesis

The diagnosis led to a clear commercial choice.

We could continue relying heavily on direct acquisition in a market with larger competitors and limited brand awareness.

Or we could build additional routes to customers.

I chose the second.

The model needed to combine:

**Direct Acquisition + B2B2C + Strategic Partnerships + Telecom + Lifecycle Management**

Each part had a different role.

Direct acquisition allowed us to reach consumers ourselves.

Strategic partnerships gave us access to established customer bases.

B2B2C allowed partners to incorporate Nextory into their own customer propositions.

Telecom created another potential route to large-scale distribution.

Lifecycle management helped us create more value from customers after acquisition.

The objective was diversification.

If one acquisition channel became expensive or underperformed, the business should not depend entirely on it.

But there was another important principle.

A partnership was only useful if it produced customers and commercial value.

The objective was not to accumulate logos.

It was to build distribution.

---

## 05. Build the Partnership Engine

Nextory already had relationships with Grupo Planeta and Telefónica through the Nubico acquisition.

Those relationships provided a useful starting point.

But they were not enough to build the scale we wanted.

We needed to expand the partnership base.

I approached partnerships as a commercial channel.

The first question was not:

**Who can we partner with?**

It was:

**Who already has access to large groups of customers that could be relevant to Nextory?**

That opened several sectors.

We developed relationships across:

- Airlines
- Hospitality and travel
- Financial services
- Retail
- Automotive
- Telecom
- Other consumer-facing businesses

Partners developed during the scale-up included companies such as:

**Iberia | Vueling | Air Europa | Meliá | Avis | Santander | BBVA | El Corte Inglés | BMW | Renault**

These were different businesses.

But they shared something valuable.

They already had customers.

The commercial opportunity was to create propositions where Nextory could add value to those existing relationships.

That could include:

- Customer benefits
- Loyalty propositions
- Bundles
- Promotions
- Rewards
- Customer acquisition campaigns
- Co-marketing
- Distribution agreements

The specific structure depended on the partner.

The principle did not.

> **Instead of paying to find every customer ourselves, we could work with organizations that already had access to them.**

---

## 06. Turn Partnerships Into Distribution

Signing a partnership was not the objective.

Activation was.

A partnership that looked strong on paper but produced no customers had limited commercial value.

So the management logic became:

**Partner**
↓
**Customer Access**
↓
**Offer**
↓
**Activation**
↓
**Customer**
↓
**Engagement**
↓
**Retention**
↓
**Commercial Value**

This changed how partnerships were evaluated.

The relevant questions became:

How many customers can the partner reach?

Is the audience relevant?

What is the customer proposition?

How will the offer be communicated?

Who owns activation?

What does the customer need to do?

How do we measure conversion?

What happens after acquisition?

Does the partnership create repeatable value?

That required coordination across commercial, marketing, product, and operations.

The partner opened the door.

We still needed to convert that access into customers.

Over time, partnerships became a major part of the Spanish commercial model.

Approximately **62% of annual revenue was ultimately generated through enterprise and telecom partnerships.**

That result mattered for more than revenue.

It showed that the business had created an additional acquisition engine rather than relying primarily on direct consumer marketing.

The model became more diversified.

And the partnership channel became a core part of how the Spanish business scaled.

> **The value of a partnership is not the agreement. It is the distribution it creates.**

---

## 07. Localize the GTM

Building distribution was only part of the scale-up.

The commercial proposition also needed to work for the Spanish market.

Localization could not mean simply translating a global campaign.

We needed to understand how customers discovered the product, what they valued, how they consumed digital content, what influenced purchase, and what created friction.

That affected:

- Positioning
- Customer segments
- Acquisition channels
- Partner propositions
- Pricing
- Packaging
- Promotions
- Customer communication
- Onboarding
- Retention

The objective was to use Nextory's global product and capabilities while adapting the commercial execution to the local market.

The principle was simple:

> **Keep the strength of the global product. Build the commercial model around the reality of the local customer.**

---

## 08. Redesign Pricing & Packaging

Pricing and packaging were part of the customer proposition.

The inherited model needed adjustment.

We reviewed how the offer matched local customer behavior and how different packages could improve both adoption and customer value.

The objective was not simply to increase price.

It was to create a better balance between:

**Customer Value + Adoption + Revenue Quality + Margin**

The changes produced measurable improvements.

**Average Contract Value increased approximately 35%.**

**Gross margin improved approximately 12%.**

Those results mattered because they showed that scale did not need to come only from adding more customers.

The economics of each customer relationship could also improve.

> **Growth is stronger when the customer proposition and the business economics improve together.**

---

## 09. Improve Customer Acquisition Economics

The partnership strategy also changed how we thought about acquisition.

Direct digital acquisition remained important.

But it no longer needed to carry the entire growth burden.

By creating additional routes to customers through established brands, the acquisition mix became broader.

Customer acquisition cost improved by approximately:

**46%**

The important point was not simply that CAC decreased.

It was why.

We were changing the system around acquisition:

**Channel Mix + Partnerships + Proposition + Conversion + Customer Value**

That created a better management question.

Instead of asking:

**How do we buy customers more cheaply?**

We could ask:

> **What is the most effective way to reach and acquire the right customer?**

Sometimes the answer was direct acquisition.

Sometimes it was a partner.

Sometimes it was a bundle or promotion.

The objective was to use the route that made the strongest commercial sense.

---

## 10. Improve Retention & Customer Value

Acquisition alone would not create a strong subscription business.

Customers needed to stay.

At the starting point, annual churn was approximately:

**42%**

That was too high.

The business needed a stronger connection with customers after acquisition.

Lifecycle automation became part of the solution.

We introduced more structured onboarding and customer engagement flows designed to help users activate, engage with the product, and continue using it.

The logic was:

**Acquisition**
↓
**Onboarding**
↓
**Activation**
↓
**Engagement**
↓
**Habit**
↓
**Retention**
↓
**Customer Value**

Over time:

**Churn decreased from approximately 42% to 29%.**

**Customer Lifetime Value approximately doubled.**

This changed the commercial equation.

A customer acquired efficiently but lost quickly creates limited value.

A customer who activates, engages, and stays creates a much stronger business.

That is why acquisition and retention could not be managed separately.

---

## 11. Build the Local Commercial Capability

A stronger commercial model required a local team capable of executing it.

The Spanish operation started with a very small local structure.

As the business developed, we expanded the local capability around the work that needed to be done.

That included:

- Marketing
- Partnership development
- Sales development
- Commercial execution
- Administrative support

The principle was not to build a large organization.

It was to build the capabilities required by the commercial model.

As the partnership pipeline grew, we needed people who could develop opportunities, activate partners, coordinate execution, and follow performance.

As customer acquisition expanded, marketing needed stronger local execution.

As the operation became more complex, the supporting processes also needed to mature.

The team therefore grew with the business.

But adding people was only part of the answer.

Roles needed clear ownership.

Commercial priorities needed to be understood.

Partnerships needed follow-through.

And different functions needed to work against the same customer and growth objectives.

> **The objective was not to build headcount. It was to build capability.**

---

## 12. Build the Commercial Operating System

As the number of channels, partners, customers, and activities increased, the business needed more structure.

The commercial model had several moving parts:

**Direct Acquisition**

**Partnerships**

**B2B2C Distribution**

**Customer Activation**

**Retention**

**Pricing & Packaging**

Managing these separately would make it difficult to understand what was actually driving growth.

We therefore needed visibility across the customer journey.

The questions were practical:

How much demand are we creating?

Where is it coming from?

Which partners are producing customers?

Which channels are converting?

What does acquisition cost?

Are new customers activating?

Are they staying?

Which propositions are working?

Where are we losing customers?

What needs attention now?

The objective was not more reporting.

It was to connect commercial activity with management decisions.

The operating logic became:

**Activity**
↓
**KPI**
↓
**Result**
↓
**Diagnosis**
↓
**Decision**
↓
**Action**

That applied across direct acquisition, partnerships, conversion, activation, and retention.

It also created a common commercial view across the local team.

Marketing could see what happened after acquisition.

Partnership development could see whether agreements produced customers.

Customer lifecycle performance could influence acquisition decisions.

And management could see which parts of the commercial engine deserved more attention.

> **Scale requires visibility. If you cannot see what is driving the result, it becomes difficult to repeat it.**

---

## 13. Learn and Adjust

The commercial model was not built perfectly on day one.

Some channels worked better than others.

Some partnerships created stronger customer access.

Some propositions converted better.

Some acquisition routes produced better economics.

Customer behavior also provided information that could not be learned from a plan alone.

The operating approach therefore needed a simple loop:

**Build → Launch → Measure → Learn → Adjust**

When a partnership worked, we looked at why.

When activation was weak, we examined the proposition and customer journey.

When acquisition became expensive, we looked at channel mix.

When churn remained high, we looked beyond acquisition and into onboarding and engagement.

When a commercial assumption did not hold, we changed it.

The objective was not to defend the original plan.

It was to build a model that worked.

> **Execution was not the final step of the strategy. It was how we learned what the strategy needed to become.**

> **In a subscription business, winning the customer is only the beginning.**

## 16. What This Case Proves

This case demonstrates a different type of executive challenge from a turnaround.

The product already existed.

The market already existed.

The acquisition had already created an initial position.

The challenge was to build what was missing around it.

### Commercial Scale-Up

Taking a small local commercial base and building the capabilities required to scale it.

### GTM

Understanding that a global product still requires a commercial model adapted to the local market.

### Partnership Strategy

Identifying organizations with relevant customer access and turning those relationships into productive distribution.

### B2B2C

Using established brands and customer ecosystems as additional routes to market rather than depending primarily on direct acquisition.

### Distribution

Building multiple customer acquisition routes across telecom, travel, banking, retail, automotive, and direct channels.

### Customer Economics

Connecting acquisition cost, pricing, customer value, retention, and LTV rather than optimizing each metric separately.

### Subscription Business

Understanding that growth depends on both acquiring customers and keeping them engaged over time.

### Pricing & Packaging

Using the commercial proposition as a lever for adoption, customer value, and stronger economics.

### Team Building

Building the local capabilities required by the business rather than adding headcount without a clear purpose.

### Commercial Operations

Creating enough visibility across channels, customers, partnerships, activation, and retention to make better decisions.

### Hands-On Execution

Moving from partnership negotiation and GTM decisions into customer journeys, channel performance, pricing, acquisition economics, and local execution when required.

### Building

Taking an operation with limited local commercial infrastructure and building the commercial engine around an existing product.

That is the central point of this case.

> **I did not need to invent the product. I needed to build the system that allowed the product to scale.**

## 17. What I Would Carry Forward

A few principles from this experience remain relevant to how I think about commercial scale.

### Do not confuse market presence with a scalable business.

Being in a market does not mean the commercial engine is built.

### Do not depend on one route to the customer.

Channel concentration creates risk.

### Look for existing customer access.

Sometimes the most efficient path to customers is through organizations that already serve them.

### Treat partnerships as distribution.

A signed agreement has little value if it does not activate customers.

### Localize the commercial model, not only the language.

Pricing, packaging, channels, customer behavior, and partnerships can all require local adaptation.

### Manage acquisition and retention together.

Growth becomes expensive when customers leave too quickly.

### Build capability around the model.

The team should reflect what the business actually needs to execute.

### Use execution to learn.

The first plan is rarely the final model.

---

## The Operating Principle

If I had to reduce this scale-up to one idea:

> **When direct customer acquisition is expensive, do not automatically spend more. Ask who already has access to the customers you want.**

Then build a proposition that creates value for:

- The partner
- The customer
- The business

Distribution can become an advantage when it is designed as part of the commercial system rather than treated as a collection of relationships.
