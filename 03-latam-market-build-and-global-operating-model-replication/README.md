# Crimson Education | LATAM Market Build & Operating Model Replication

Building a multi-market education business from a limited regional base and creating a repeatable model for expansion.

**Company:** Crimson Education  
**Role:** Country Manager → Chief Operating Officer  
**Geography:** LATAM  
**Business:** International education, university admissions consulting, tutoring, mentoring, and online education  
**Mandate:** Build and scale the LATAM commercial operation  
**Focus:** Market Expansion | GTM | Localization | Partnerships | Sales | Customer Acquisition | Commercial Operations | Operating Model

---

## Executive Snapshot

I joined Crimson to build and scale its LATAM operation.

The starting point was limited.

Brazil and Mexico already existed, but there was no established regional operating model.

There was no regional GTM.

There was no local team across the new priority markets.

Pricing had not been adapted for the region.

Institutional partnerships had not been developed.

Marketing was being run from Europe in English.

Brand awareness was very low.

The commercial engine was small and inconsistent.

But the product itself was strong.

Crimson already had a differentiated education proposition, global expertise, and a clear customer outcome.

The challenge was building the regional system required to take that product to market.

That meant answering practical questions:

Which markets should we prioritize?

Who was the right customer?

How should we position the proposition locally?

What should remain consistent across LATAM?

What needed to change by market?

How could we build trust quickly?

Which acquisition channels could scale?

What role should schools, universities, and corporations play?

How should the sales motion work?

What local capabilities did we need?

And how could we expand without building a completely different operating model in every country?

The objective was not simply to open markets.

It was to find a model that could be localized, measured, improved, and repeated.

The operating logic became:

**Prioritize**

**Localize**  
↓  
**Build Trust**

**Create Distribution**

Elements of the model were later applied to other international markets when I moved into a broader commercial role.

**Building the LATAM engine.**

> **The challenge was not opening more countries. It was building a model that could work across them.**

## 02. The Starting Point

Crimson already had a strong education proposition and an established international business.

LATAM was different.

Brazil and Mexico had existing operations, but there was no broader regional model for building and connecting the rest of LATAM.

Across the priority markets, the starting point included:

- No established regional GTM
- No local team
- No localized pricing model
- No defined regional ICP
- No institutional partnership network
- Limited brand awareness
- Marketing managed remotely from Europe
- English-language messaging that did not reflect the local customer
- Weak lead generation
- Limited funnel discipline
- Long sales cycles
- High customer acquisition costs
- Inconsistent CRM usage
- No consistent regional operating rhythm

The business had some historical customer activity in the region.

But it did not yet have the infrastructure required to scale across multiple markets.

That distinction mattered.

Selling to customers in a country is not the same as having a market operation.

And having several individual markets does not automatically create a regional business.

The challenge was to build the layer connecting them.

**Regional Priorities**  
↓  
**Local Market Execution**  
↓  
**Shared GTM**  
↓  
**Common Commercial Standards**  
↓  
**Local Distribution**  
↓  
**Sales Execution**  
↓  
**Customer Delivery**  
↓  
**Performance Management**

The objective was to create enough consistency to scale without ignoring the differences between markets.

## 03. The Expansion Problem

The easiest approach would have been to treat every country as a separate launch.

I did not believe that would scale.

Building a different GTM, commercial process, pricing logic, team structure, and management system for every country would create unnecessary complexity.

But the opposite approach was equally problematic.

LATAM could not be treated as one homogeneous market.

Customer behavior differed.

Institutional relationships differed.

Market maturity differed.

Competitive dynamics differed.

Trust and brand awareness differed.

The challenge was therefore finding the right balance between:

**Standardization**

and

**Localization**

Some elements needed regional consistency.

Others needed local judgment.

The operating question became:

> **What should we build once, and what should we adapt by market?**

That question shaped the expansion model.

### Build Once

Where possible, we wanted common commercial infrastructure around:

- ICP logic
- Sales methodology
- Funnel stages
- CRM discipline
- Response standards
- Qualification
- Core KPIs
- Pricing principles
- Management cadence
- Partnership methodology

### Adapt Locally

The market-facing execution needed greater flexibility around:

- Messaging
- Customer context
- Partnership targets
- Institutional relationships
- Demand generation
- Market sequencing
- Local credibility
- Commercial conversations
- Channel emphasis

This created a practical expansion philosophy:

**Standardize the system. Localize the execution.**

The first major decision was therefore not how to launch every country.

It was where to start.

We prioritized Colombia, Peru, and Chile as the first expansion wave.

The logic was based on factors including:

- Demand for international education
- Concentration of internationally oriented schools
- Access to the target customer
- Potential for institutional partnerships
- Ability to build early commercial traction

Rather than spreading limited resources across the entire region, we concentrated execution where we believed we could learn and gain traction faster.

Those first markets would help us test the model.

What worked could then be strengthened.

What failed could be changed.

And what proved repeatable could be applied to the next market.

> **Expansion becomes easier when every new market starts with what the previous market already taught you.**

---

## 04. Build the Regional GTM

Once the first markets were prioritized, the next challenge was building the GTM.

The existing approach was not designed for LATAM.

Marketing was being managed from Europe.

Messaging was in English.

The regional ICP was not sufficiently defined.

Brand awareness was low.

And the commercial proposition needed to make sense to local families and institutions.

We needed to build the regional demand engine.

Within the first 90 days, we developed the LATAM marketing foundation around:

- Localized Spanish messaging
- Clearer ICP definition
- Regional segmentation
- Webinar-based demand generation
- Local market campaigns
- Stronger connection between marketing and sales

The objective was not simply localization of content.

It was localization of the commercial motion.

The customer needed to understand:

Why Crimson?

Why now?

Why is this relevant to me?

Why should I trust the company?

And why should I make a significant education decision with a brand that was still relatively unknown in my market?

That last question mattered.

Crimson was selling a high-consideration education service.

Trust was therefore part of the GTM.

We needed more than advertising.

We needed credibility.

That led directly to the second part of the model.

---

## 05. Lead With Partnerships

I made partnerships one of the primary routes to market.

The logic was straightforward.

Crimson had limited brand awareness.

But the students and families we wanted to reach were already connected to institutions they trusted.

That included:

- Private K12 schools
- Universities
- Multinational employers

Instead of trying to build trust only through direct marketing, we could build relationships with organizations that already had credibility with our target customers.

The model became:

**Crimson**  
↓  
**Trusted Institution**  
↓  
**Student / Family**  
↓  
**Commercial Opportunity**

Schools gave us access to internationally oriented students and families.

Universities created institutional credibility and access to relevant communities.

Multinational employers created another route through employee and family benefit propositions.

The objective was not simply to sign partnerships.

It was to create:

**Trust + Access + Qualified Demand + Distribution**

We built relationships across leading schools, universities, and multinational employers.

Over time, the partnership pipeline generated approximately **30% to 40% of SQL flow**.

That made partnerships a meaningful part of the acquisition engine.

It also reduced the need to build the market entirely through paid B2C acquisition.

> **When brand awareness is low, trusted distribution can accelerate both credibility and demand.**

---

## 06. Build the Regional Sales Motion

Generating demand was not enough.

The commercial process also needed to convert it.

At the starting point, the sales cycle could take approximately three to four months.

SQL flow was weak.

CRM discipline was inconsistent.

And there was no sufficiently standardized regional sales motion.

We rebuilt the commercial process around clearer stages and expectations.

That included:

- SDR coverage across the region
- 24-hour response targets
- Structured customer assessments
- Clearer qualification
- More consistent funnel management
- Stronger CRM discipline
- Regional sales standards
- Better connection between demand generation and sales execution

The objective was to create a sales motion that could be taught and repeated.

The basic commercial flow became:

**Lead**  
↓  
**Response**  
↓  
**Qualification**  
↓  
**Assessment**  
↓  
**Opportunity**  
↓  
**Commercial Conversation**  
↓  
**Decision**  
↓  
**Customer**

Each stage needed greater clarity.

Who owns it?

What needs to happen?

What qualifies progression?

Where are we losing customers?

How quickly are we moving?

What does the CRM tell us?

What needs management attention?

The improvements were material.

**SQL flow increased approximately 50% within six months.**

**Conversion increased approximately 3x.**

**The sales cycle decreased from approximately three to four months to approximately three to four weeks.**

These results were connected.

Better targeting improved demand quality.

Partnerships created additional qualified demand.

Faster response improved customer engagement.

Structured assessments improved qualification.

A clearer sales process reduced friction.

CRM discipline improved visibility.

The commercial engine became easier to manage and easier to replicate.

> **A regional sales model only scales when another person can understand it, execute it, and produce a comparable customer experience.**

## 07. Standardize What Should Scale

As the regional model developed, we needed consistency.

If every market created its own pricing logic, sales process, qualification standards, CRM practices, and KPIs, the organization would become harder to manage with every new country.

So we standardized the parts of the commercial system where consistency created leverage.

That included:

- Pricing principles
- ICP logic
- Sales stages
- Qualification
- Response standards
- CRM discipline
- Core commercial KPIs
- Partnership methodology
- Management expectations

Pricing was an important example.

Rather than creating unnecessary pricing complexity across LATAM, we moved toward a more standardized regional approach.

The objective was practical:

**Protect Margin**  
↓  
**Simplify Commercial Execution**  
↓  
**Reduce Friction**  
↓  
**Make the Model Easier to Replicate**

Standardization did not mean ignoring local market conditions.

It meant avoiding complexity where complexity added little value.

> **If something does not need to be different by market, do not make it different.**

---

## 08. Localize What the Customer Experiences

The operating system could be standardized.

The customer experience could not simply be copied from one country to another.

Local execution still needed to reflect:

- Language
- Customer expectations
- Education systems
- Institutional relationships
- Market maturity
- Parent concerns
- Competitive context
- Local channels
- Trust

This distinction became important.

We did not need a completely different business model for every country.

But we did need enough local understanding to make the common model relevant.

That allowed the region to share commercial infrastructure while still responding to local market reality.

## 09. Improve the Customer Economics

The commercial model also needed stronger economics.

At the starting point, customer acquisition cost was high.

The answer was not one isolated cost reduction initiative.

We worked across the system.

Better ICP definition improved targeting.

Localized GTM improved relevance.

Partnerships created additional qualified demand.

Faster response improved engagement.

A clearer sales motion improved conversion.

Stronger onboarding and delivery improved the customer relationship after acquisition.

The combined result was:

**Customer acquisition cost reduced approximately 35%.**

**Customer lifetime value approximately doubled.**

**Referral activity increased approximately 200%.**

These metrics were connected.

A better customer experience created more value after the sale.

Better retention and longer customer relationships improved LTV.

Satisfied families created referrals.

Referrals created another source of demand.

The commercial system therefore extended beyond acquisition:

**Acquire**  
↓  
**Convert**  
↓  
**Deliver**  
↓  
**Create Value**  
↓  
**Retain**  
↓  
**Generate Advocacy**

**Create New Demand**

> **The strongest acquisition engine eventually includes customers who are willing to recommend you.**

## 10. Build the Delivery Capability Around Growth

Commercial growth creates another problem if the organization cannot deliver what it sells.

As student volume increased, the delivery model needed to scale with it.

The business had multiple components across the student journey, including:

- Admissions consulting
- Academic tutoring
- Mentoring
- Academic planning
- Online education through CGA
- Ongoing student and family support

Commercial execution and delivery therefore could not operate independently.

The sales process needed to set the right expectations.

The customer needed a clear onboarding experience.

Delivery needed visibility into the students entering the system.

And customer feedback needed to inform the commercial model.

We strengthened the connection between acquisition and delivery so that growth did not come at the expense of the customer experience.

This contributed to stronger customer value and referral performance.

It also reinforced an important principle:

**Revenue quality matters as much as revenue acquisition.**

A commercial organization can create short-term growth by selling aggressively.

A scalable education business needs to acquire customers it can serve well.

> **Do not scale demand faster than the organization can deliver the promise behind it.**

## 13. Build the Regional Organization

The operating model also required people.

As the region developed, we built local and regional capability across the commercial organization.

That included:

- Sales
- SDR
- Marketing
- Partnerships
- Customer-facing roles
- Supporting commercial operations

The objective was not to reproduce a complete organization in every country.

We needed to determine which capabilities should sit regionally and which needed to be closer to the customer.

That required balancing:

**Regional Leverage**

with

**Local Market Knowledge**

Some capabilities benefited from common standards and regional coordination.

Others required stronger local presence, particularly where customer relationships, partnerships, and market understanding mattered.

The organization therefore developed around the operating model.

Not the other way around.

Roles needed clear ownership.

Markets needed enough autonomy to respond locally.

Regional leadership needed enough visibility to identify what was working across countries.

And teams needed common commercial standards so performance could be understood and compared.

> **Do not build the organization chart first. Build the operating model, then determine the capabilities required to run it.**

## 18. What I Would Carry Forward

Several principles from this experience continue to shape how I approach international expansion.

### Prioritize before expanding.

More markets do not automatically create more growth.

Concentrate resources where the probability of traction is strongest.

### Do not confuse translation with localization.

A translated campaign can still be commercially irrelevant.

Understand the customer and the market.

### Build trust deliberately.

In high-consideration categories, credibility is part of the acquisition model.

### Use institutions as distribution when it makes sense.

The right partner can provide both customer access and trust.

### Standardize what creates leverage.

Sales methodology, CRM discipline, KPIs, qualification, and operating standards should not be reinvented unnecessarily.

### Localize what creates relevance.

Customer conversations, partnerships, channels, and market execution often require local judgment.

### Connect acquisition with delivery.

Growth is not sustainable if the customer experience cannot support it.

### Learn from every market.

Expansion should create organizational knowledge.

### Do not rebuild from zero.

The next market should benefit from everything already learned.

---

## The Operating Principle

The principle behind the LATAM build, in one line:

> **Standardize the system. Localize the execution.**

Build the commercial infrastructure once where possible.

Then adapt it to the realities of each market.

Measure what happens.

Keep what works.

Change what does not.

Carry the learning into the next market.

That is how expansion becomes more repeatable.
